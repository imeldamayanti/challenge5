## Participant snapshot

Dea is a tourism graduate who returned to guiding after Covid, when Bali reopened. She works freelance across 4–5 travel agents — some as a tour guide, some doing airport transfers, some collecting large groups at the airport. She chose guiding for the flexible hours; she dislikes office / 9-to-5 work.

Guide licensing in Indonesia is per-province and runs through HPI (Himpunan Pramuwisata Indonesia): roughly two years of supervised work under a company's license before a guide can apply for their own. This year's English-guide quota was cut, so applications were postponed to next year. Kultara, being a social business rather than a formal travel agency, does not require a licensed guide — it runs its own storyteller training through "Kultara Academy."

## What the trips look like

Kultara open trips run on weekends, in Indonesian, on fixed pre-scheduled routes (walking or cycling). Private trips are customised and far more mixed. Across her other agencies, Dea also runs 4-hour cultural trips, temple-focused tours, and palace/kingdom tours around Bali and the smaller islands (Nusa Penida, Ceningan, Lembongan).

## Who the tourists are

Kultara open trips are mostly Indonesian; Kultara private trips are highly mixed — Australia, Singapore, Hong Kong, couples, friend groups, even whole university classes. Her other agencies skew differently: American retirees plus Polish guests at a cultural village; German and Dutch guests at the airport. Repeat guests make up roughly 20–30% at Kultara (usually taking a different route the second time); the rest are first-timers. B2B agencies bring repeat guests from the same country, sometimes returning after 2–3 years with friends.

## Behaviour patterns

**By age.** Teens show low interest in history and explanation — school trips are booked by teachers, not students, so buy-in is low. Adults and anyone who self-books tend to be highly interested because they've already researched the route. Older guests avoid heavy walking: they prefer food tours, sitting and listening while chatting, and visiting only 1–3 spots.

**Local vs international.** Little behavioural difference — both groups are already interested in the place. The main tell is that Indonesians handle heat poorly, so they choose early-morning or late-afternoon routes.

**Engagement peaks when guests DO something.** Hands-on activities — wood carving, plowing a rice field with cattle, dancing with local kids — are what guests enjoy and remember most. In Dea's words, "when something moves, or they're in charge of doing it, that's the most interesting." Highlights guests recalled from a recent Sanur trip were dancing with the kids, the museum, and the turtles.

**Questions.** Foreigners ask "what's this? what's that?" about new things as they walk. Indonesians, who already know Bali, ask to validate their curiosity or something a friend told them. Tourists pre-search on Google, but only superficially — they rely on the guide for detail.

## How Dea handles disengagement

She throws questions back to the group or opens the floor for questions. If a guest has clearly lost interest, she lets them be and focuses on the engaged ones. Photo-takers are fine as long as they stay with the group, and guests wandering or on their phones during free time is "no problem, as long as they're not bored." Boredom typically shows up when someone books with a friend but only one of them is actually interested — the other is just tagging along.

## Friction & pain points

Heritage sites can close last-minute for Balinese ceremonies; the options are to skip the stop or divert to a nearby alternative. Guests can't swap a destination just because they dislike it — destinations are pre-decided by the participant/admin, so the only option is to skip to the next one. The most common complaint is the route itself — tired, too far, too winding. Open trips draw complaints when bookings are low and the group feels empty; guests want a lively atmosphere with more people, whereas private trips rarely complain because they get full attention. Detailed permit/licensing questions fall outside a guide's authority to answer. And while Dea preps tickets or bikes, guests are left with idle "free time" — they wander, take photos, or check their phones (at Kultara the bikes are pre-prepared to keep the wait short).

## Opportunities

Route design is mostly tuned early. The Sanur route was originally longer and more winding; feedback asked for more cycling time, so it was shortened. Routes are now fixed, so ongoing changes are minimal — meaning experience improvements have to come from *layering content and interaction*, not re-routing. Notably, the interview's design brief explicitly probed for a "helper tool" to fill guests' idle/free time and make the experience more engaging — signalling the team's prototype direction toward some form of self-guided content for unguided moments.
